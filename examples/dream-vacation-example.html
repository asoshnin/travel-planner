Route Overview

Pisa (fly in) → Vernazza, Cinque Terre (2 nights) → San Gimignano, Tuscany (2 nights) → Positano, Amalfi Coast (2 nights) → Naples (fly out)

Why this route works: it runs north to south in one direction, so there's no backtracking — each leg is a genuine step toward the airport you're leaving from. Cinque Terre first, while the legs are freshest, since its best moments reward walking, not driving. Tuscany in the middle absorbs the longest single drive as a full travel day with a wine-region lunch break built in. Amalfi goes last and closest to Naples airport, because that coast road is the one place on this trip worth being rested for, not rushed through.

Total driving across the trip: roughly 6.5–7 hours, split across two travel days — never more than about 4 hours in one sitting.

Day 1–2 · Vernazza, Cinque Terre

Staying at: Vernazza

View on OpenStreetMap →

Getting here: Land in Pisa, pick up the rental car, and aim to leave the airport by 10:30 AM — Vernazza's own parking is small and fills fast, so earlier is genuinely better. Drive north (about 1 hr 15 to the La Spezia area; allow a 20–30 min buffer for airport-lot traffic and the last stretch of coastal road).
Parking: Vernassoa Parking — guarded, shuttle to the village roughly 8 AM–8 PM, ~€2.50/hour or ~€20/day. Fills up in summer; fallback is parking in Monterosso, Levanto, or La Spezia and taking the train in.

Vernazza is the one with the harbor everyone photographs — a tight cluster of pastel buildings wrapped around a natural breakwater, with the ruins of a 14th-century watchtower over the water. Base here rather than in Monterosso (bigger, busier) or Riomaggiore (better nightlife, worse for an early one).

Day 1: Arrive, settle in, walk the harbor at golden hour. Dinner: Ristorante Belforte, built into the old fortress on the cliff edge — widely considered the best table in Cinque Terre, ~€35–60/head. Closed Tuesdays; reservations genuinely necessary — book ahead.
Day 2: The full "do nothing" day. Either the Sentiero Azzurro trail section toward Corniglia (check open/closed status locally — sections close for maintenance and after storm damage more often than people expect), or the climb up to Doria Castle, the watchtower ruins above the harbor (summer hours, June–Sept, roughly 11 AM–7 PM, ~€2.20 admission, but shift with season/weather — confirm locally). Dinner in Vernazza again rather than train-hopping for one more town — this is the relaxed-pace request in action.

Day 3–4 · San Gimignano, Tuscany

Staying at: San Gimignano

View on OpenStreetMap →

Getting here: Recommended departure from the La Spezia/Vernazza area: 10:30 AM, to arrive by early afternoon. Drive south (about 2 hr 45 with a stop; allow a 20-min buffer) into the Val d'Elsa. San Gimignano is nicknamed "the medieval Manhattan" for the 14 surviving stone towers (from an original 72) that noble families built centuries ago purely to outdo each other — a UNESCO World Heritage historic center that looks like a skyline from the 1300s.
Parking: Parcheggio Giubileo (P1) — the official municipal lot, a few hundred meters south of the center. €2.00/hour (first two hours), €1.50/hour after, €7.00/24 hours. A shuttle bus (April–October, ~€1 round trip) connects it to town if you'd rather not walk.
Day 3: Arrive by early afternoon, walk the town before the day-trip coach crowds arrive at midday tomorrow (San Gimignano is a popular bus-tour stop — early morning and evening are noticeably quieter). Dinner: Le Vecchie Mura, built into the old city walls with panoramic Val d'Elsa views — wild boar ragu or a T-bone, with a glass of Vernaccia di San Gimignano, the town's own DOCG white. Dinner only, roughly 6:00–9:45 PM, Wed–Mon; closed Tuesdays. Book ahead in peak season.
Day 4: Climb Torre Grossa, the tallest surviving tower (the only one open to visitors) and its civic museum. Hours: 9 AM–7 PM (Apr–Sep), 11 AM–5 PM (Oct–Mar), last entry 30 min before close — €9, or a €15 two-day pass covering all civic museums. Then a half-day out into the countryside — a small vineyard visit near the town (many take walk-ins, some require booking a day ahead) — then back for the "do nothing" half: a slow lunch, an afternoon in the shade of the Piazza della Cisterna.

Day 5–6 · Positano, Amalfi Coast

Staying at: Positano

View on OpenStreetMap →

Getting here: This is the leg where the departure time is a safety decision, not a convenience one. Recommended departure from San Gimignano: 11:00 AM, to arrive Positano by mid-afternoon — well before dusk, with margin to spare. The drive itself is the longest of the trip (about 3 hr 15, more with summer traffic; build in a 30–45 min buffer), and the one genuinely demanding stretch: the SS163 coast road is narrow, cliff-hugging, and has blind hairpin turns with tour buses coming the other way. This is the one leg where "not comfortable driving narrow cliff roads at night" actually matters — a smaller/compact rental is a real asset here, not just a preference. There's no toll road shortcut around this — the SS163 is the route.
Parking: Positano's center is car-free, and there's no single unified municipal parking site the way San Gimignano has — each lot is independently run. The two closest to the center are De Gennaro and Mandara, both around €8–10/hour. (Source: Locauto, Positano.com) Leave the car at whichever has space and walk (or take the local bus/boat) from there.

Positano itself is a vertical town — houses stacked up the cliffside above a small beach, no flat ground to speak of.

Day 5: Arrive, settle in, walk down to the beach for a late-afternoon swim. Dinner: Ristorante Bruno, a cliff-hugging terrace with sunset views over the town and sea, family-run since 1959 — worth booking specifically for that view, 3–4 weeks ahead in peak season. Hours weren't consistently confirmed across sources (reports range roughly 11:30 AM–11:30 PM, no clear weekly closure found) — confirm via the official booking page.
Day 6: The do-nothing day, properly — a half-day boat trip along the coast (weather permitting) is the local way to see Positano the way it's actually meant to be seen: from the water, looking up. For a quieter morning stop, Chiesa di Santa Maria Assunta — the church with Positano's landmark majolica-tiled dome — is open daily, 9:30 AM–noon and 4–8 PM, free entry (small fee only for the crypt). Go early morning or late afternoon; it hosts weddings and services on weekends. Afternoon free for the beach or the shopping streets. Recommended departure the next morning: 9:00 AM for the ~1-hour drive to Naples airport — adjust to your flight time, and separately budget a ~2-hour arrival cushion (distinct from the drive-time buffer) for rental drop-off, the parking-lot walk, and airport processing.

Practical Notes

  • Driving: an automatic-transmission compact car, booked well ahead — automatics are less common and cost more in Italy, and a compact matters specifically for the Amalfi Coast leg.
  • Parking: none of the town centers on this route allow cars in; every stop above has a named, linked parking option and a fallback if it's full.
  • ZTL zones: Positano and San Gimignano both have restricted traffic zones (ZTL) in their historic cores — driving into one without authorization risks an automatic fine by camera. Confirm your hotel's guidance before driving in.
  • Timing the Amalfi leg: deliberately scheduled to arrive before dusk, given the stated driver comfort constraint — see the departure-time recommendation above.
  • Reservations: book Ristorante Belforte and Ristorante Bruno ahead, not on arrival — both genuinely need it in peak season.
  • Payment/connectivity: no time zone change or border crossing on this route; card payment is standard at all named venues above, though it's worth carrying some euros for smaller cash-preferred spots.
